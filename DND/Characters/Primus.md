God of Order, Law, Structure, Inevitability, and Mathematics

Using the [[Muryland Device]] to create a portal to correct Nyr

Primus is the god of Order, Law, Structure, Civilization, Mathematics, and Inevitability. He is the architect-deity whose first act was to speak the law that a thing cannot both be and not be — the axiom from which all subsequent reality unfolded. He stands apart from the other gods not out of aloofness but out of category: he does not see himself as one god among many so much as the operating principle beneath all of them. People honor him with precision rather than passion; his worship is contractual, not devotional. Few pray to Primus for mercy. Many invoke him when they need a debt enforced, a contract honored, or a structure to hold. Worshipers, Clergy & Temples The church teaches that the universe is fundamentally legible — that everything, properly understood, resolves into a working equation. Suffering, in this theology, is not punishment but miscalibration: a variable that has drifted out of its proper place. The faithful do not ask Primus for comfort. They ask him for correctness. One of the central tenets of faith is that a well-ordered life is a moral life. The faithful believe that chaos is not merely inconvenient but corrosive — that every unkept promise, every abandoned structure, every neglected debt is a small tear in the fabric that Primus first spoke into being. Followers are taught to keep precise records, honor every agreement to the letter, and treat broken order as a wound requiring immediate repair. Sins: Breaking a contract, however small, is considered a genuine moral failing — not merely bad practice. Improvisation in matters that ought to be planned is viewed with deep suspicion. Worse than any single broken promise, however, is the deliberate introduction of disorder for its own sake — chaos performed as entertainment, rebellion without structural purpose, the cultivation of randomness. Followers of Primus consider this the closest thing their faith has to true evil, and they reserve a particular, cold contempt for it. Worshippers Primus has comparatively few devoted worshippers among ordinary mortals — his theology does not comfort the grieving or promise rescue to the desperate — but his clergy are disproportionately influential. Guild masters, magistrates, architects, accountants, and the keepers of any institution that depends on enforceable structure tend to find their way to him eventually, whether or not they call it faith. Offerings to Primus are rarely emotional. They take the form of completed work: a finished ledger, a fulfilled contract, a building raised exactly to its specifications and not a stone out of true. His temples accept these as tribute in a way that other gods would consider strange. Clergy Priests of Primus are called Calibrators, and they are trained from a young age to think in systems. They do not perform miracles of healing or protection in the way other clergies do; their primary divine gift is certainty — the ability to know, with total confidence, whether a structure (a law, a building, a contract, a plan) will hold under the conditions it claims to withstand. Petitioners come to them not for blessings but for audits. Calibrators are trained to view emotional appeals with deep suspicion. A Calibrator asked to rule on a dispute will request documentation before testimony. This has earned the priesthood a reputation for coldness that the priests themselves do not dispute — they consider warmth a variable, not a virtue. Some Calibrators, troubled by what perfect order would require of a fundamentally imperfect world, leave the temple to become wandering auditors — adventurers who travel to assess whether a particular structure (a kingdom, an order, a device) is sound or dangerously miscalibrated. These wandering Calibrators are rare, and the church does not entirely trust them, since a Calibrator who has decided that a structure is unsound has also, implicitly, decided that someone built it wrong. Vestments Calibrators dress with severe precision: tailored grey or silver-white robes, perfectly pressed, with sleeves cut to an exact and unvarying length regardless of the wearer's height — a deliberate discomfort that reminds the wearer that the garment serves the system, not the body. The symbol of Primus, a perfect golden square inscribed within a circle, is worn at the collar. Senior Calibrators (the equivalent of level 7 and above) wear robes lined with a grid pattern of fine silver thread, visible only in direct light — a reminder that the structure underlying all things is always present, even when unseen. Hierarchy Advancement within the priesthood of Primus is the most literal of any faith in Nyr: a Calibrator is tested by submitting a structure of their own design — a law, a building plan, a logical proof, a piece of engineering — to a panel of senior Calibrators, who attempt for one full day to find any flaw in it. If no flaw is found, the petitioner advances. If a flaw is found, they are shown the flaw, precisely, and sent to correct it. There is no humiliation in failing this test. There is considerable humiliation in failing to address the correction. When a Calibrator is recognized by the church (usually after the equivalent of 8th level), the church funds half the cost of a new Audit House, a regional seat from which the Calibrator administers contracts, structural inspections, and disputes for their territory. Temples Temples of Primus are called Audit Houses, and they are unmistakable even to those unfamiliar with the faith: perfectly symmetrical buildings, built to exacting mathematical proportions, with no ornamentation that does not also serve a structural purpose. The central chamber contains no altar in the traditional sense — only a vast table, perfectly level, on which contracts, deeds, and disputed agreements are laid out and examined. Audit Houses double as the most reliable archives in most cities. Records kept by Primus's clergy are considered legally unimpeachable in many jurisdictions, since a Calibrator's professional reputation depends entirely on never falsifying a structure. Rituals Services to Primus are sparse and exact: the recitation of the First Law, the public reading of one resolved contract dispute from the previous month (chosen for its instructive value), and a period of silent calculation in which the congregation is invited to identify one piece of disorder in their own lives and resolve, aloud, to correct it. Primus has no holy days in the traditional sense — his clergy consider the designation of a special day to be, itself, a small irrationality, since every day is equally subject to the same laws. The closest thing to a festival is the Annual Reconciliation, held on the first day of the new year, during which every Audit House in Nyr closes its books for the previous year publicly and simultaneously, regardless of time zone or distance, a logistical feat that the clergy take immense pride in. Dogma Calibrators teach that the multiverse is, at its foundation, a solvable equation — vast and currently unsolved in its particulars, but solvable in principle. Suffering exists because the equation has not yet been correctly balanced, not because balance is impossible. This is, depending on who you ask, either the most comforting or the most chilling theology in Nyr: comforting because it implies the problem can be fixed; chilling because it implies someone is presently failing to fix it. The faithful are taught that free will is real but secondary — that mortals choose within a structure, not outside of one, and the measure of a good life is how precisely one's choices align with the structure that makes choice possible at all. Appearance, Manifestations Primus has no fixed appearance, and his clergy consider the question slightly beside the point. When he is depicted at all, he is shown as a precise geometric figure — a perfect human shape constructed from straight lines and exact angles, without texture or individuating detail, as if a person had been reduced to a technical drawing. In his rare direct manifestations (recorded only twice in mortal history, both during the Eleven Months of Silver Light), witnesses describe a silver- white light with no discernible source, moving with an unnerving, perfectly even stride. Relationships & History Primus holds himself apart from the divine politics of the rest of the pantheon, viewing most inter- divine conflict as a failure of governance that a properly structured cosmos would not permit. He considers most of the other gods well-intentioned but operationally inefficient. • Panzuriel: Primus and Panzuriel are the original disagreement from which all subsequent divine conflict descends — Order against the entropy that precedes and survives it. Primus does not hate Panzuriel. He considers him an unsolved variable, which is, for Primus, very nearly worse. • Myrkul: Primus respected Myrkul's administration of death as one of the few domains in the pantheon that ran with proper structure. Myrkul's death is the event that radicalized Primus's ambitions for Nyr — proof, to him, that the entire system required direct administration. • Nyrissa: Primus believes everything should follow a perfect design. Nyrissa believes fate is a web of possibilities, not a fixed structure. They fundamentally disagree on how the universe ought to work, yet neither actively interferes with the other's affairs — a rare instance of Primus tolerating an unresolved variable indefinitely, which suggests either great respect or a problem he has not yet found a way to solve. • Tymora and Beshaba: Primus's attempt to systematize the undivided force of Luck is the act that split it into two gods. He still finds the domain of luck philosophically irritating — a gap in the law he has never closed — and neither goddess has forgiven him for the word categorize. • Bahamut: Of all the gods, Primus holds Bahamut in the closest thing he has to genuine esteem. Justice, to Primus, is simply law made moral, and he considers Bahamut's domain one of the few in the pantheon that requires no correction.
